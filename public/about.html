History of Karnataka

From ancient empires to the modern state – the story behind the places you visit.

Karnataka’s history spans more than two thousand years. Great dynasties rose here, built temples, forts and cities that still stand, and gave Kannada one of India’s oldest literary traditions. The timeline below follows that story, with places you can visit today.

  1. Ancient times – 3rd c. BCE

    Stone Age to the Mauryas

    People lived in this region from prehistoric times, leaving megalithic burial sites and rock art. In the 3rd century BCE Emperor Ashoka’s kingdom reached the north of the state – his minor rock edicts have been found near Brahmagiri and Siddapura in Chitradurga district. The Satavahanas later ruled large parts of the Deccan.

  2. c. 345 – 1000 CE

    Kadambas and Western Gangas – the first Kannada kingdoms

    The Kadambas of Banavasi (founded by Mayurasharma) are remembered as the first dynasty to make Kannada an administrative language. The Western Gangas ruled from Talakadu; in 981 CE their minister Chavundaraya had the 57-feet Gommateshwara (Bahubali) statue carved at Shravanabelagola, one of the world’s tallest monolithic statues.

  3. c. 543 – 753 CE

    Badami Chalukyas

    Under rulers like Pulakeshin II the Chalukyas controlled much of the Deccan. They experimented with temple architecture at Aihole, carved the cave temples of Badami and built the royal temples of Pattadakal, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

  4. c. 753 – 982 CE

    Rashtrakutas

    The Rashtrakutas ruled from Manyakheta (today’s Malkhed). Kannada literature blossomed – Kavirajamarga, the earliest surviving Kannada work on poetics, belongs to this age. They are also famous for the monolithic Kailasa temple at Ellora in Maharashtra.

  5. 973 – 1189 CE

    Western Chalukyas of Kalyani

    Ruling from Kalyani (Basavakalyan), they patronised poets, scholars and a distinctive temple style seen at Lakkundi and Itagi. In the 12th century Kalyani was also the home of the social reformer Basavanna and the Lingayat (Veerashaiva) movement, whose vachana poetry is a treasure of Kannada literature.

  6. c. 1026 – 1343 CE

    Hoysalas

    From Belur and Halebidu the Hoysalas, famous under Vishnuvardhana, created temples with intricate star-shaped plans and stone carving as fine as jewellery, including the Chennakeshava Temple at Belur and Hoysaleswara Temple at Halebidu. The Keshava Temple at Somanathapura completes the group. Together these were inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage in 2023.

  7. 1336 – 1565 CE

    The Vijayanagara Empire

    Founded by brothers Harihara and Bukka, Vijayanagara grew into one of the largest and richest empires in South India. Its capital at Hampi was a bustling city of markets, temples and palaces. The empire reached its peak under Krishnadevaraya (1509–1529). After the Battle of Talikota in 1565 the city was sacked and left in ruins – today a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

  8. 1347 – 1686 CE

    Bahmani and Deccan Sultanates

    The Bahmani Sultanate ruled from Gulbarga and then Bidar, leaving forts, mosques and Persian-influenced art. Its successors, the Adil Shahis of Bijapur, built the huge Gol Gumbaz (1656), whose dome is among the largest in the world, and a famous whispering gallery.

  9. 1399 – 1947

    Wadiyars of Mysore, Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan

    The Wadiyar dynasty ruled the Mysore region for centuries and made Mysuru a city of palaces, silk, sandalwood and the grand Dasara festival. In the late 18th century Hyder Ali and his son Tipu Sultan led Mysore against the British in the Anglo-Mysore Wars; Tipu died defending Srirangapatna in 1799. The Wadiyars were then restored, and under Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV (with Dewan Sir M. Visvesvaraya) Mysore became a model state with dams, industries and universities. Bengaluru was founded by Kempe Gowda in 1537.

  10. 1800s – 1947

    British rule and the freedom struggle

    Large parts of present Karnataka came under British control, split between the Bombay and Madras Presidencies, the Nizam’s Hyderabad and smaller princely states. Kodagu (Coorg) was annexed in 1834. Local heroes resisted: Rani Chennamma of Kittur defeated a British force in 1824, and her commander Sangolli Rayanna kept the fight alive. Karnataka also took part in the national freedom movement.

  11. 1 November 1956

    Unification of Karnataka

    After independence the Kannada-speaking regions that had been scattered across different rulers were brought together in the Ekikarana (unification) movement, led by many writers and leaders. The new state, called Mysore State, was formed on 1 November 1956 and renamed Karnataka on 1 November 1973. That day is celebrated every year as Kannada Rajyotsava.

  12. Today

    Modern Karnataka

    Karnataka is known for Bengaluru’s technology and start-up industry, coffee in Kodagu and Chikkamagaluru, classical music and dance, and a rich literary tradition – Kannada has been honoured with eight Jnanpith Awards and was given classical language status in 2008. The state has three UNESCO cultural World Heritage Sites (Hampi, Pattadakal and the Hoysala temples), part of the Western Ghats natural site, and a long Arabian Sea coastline.
